What is a Game? Strategic Foundations

A mathematical framework for modeling interactive decision-making between rational agents:

The Core Vocabulary of Any Game
  1. Players (i \in N): The decision-makers (e.g. Row & Column, or Algorithm & Adversary).
  2. Action / Move (a_i \in A_i): Choices available at a given decision node.
  3. Strategy (s_i \in S_i): A complete contingency plan for every situation.
  4. Payoffs / Utility (u_i(s_1, \dots, s_k)): Numerical rewards resulting from joint actions.
  5. Information Structure: Perfect (all history known) vs. Imperfect (hidden state).
  • Fundamental Tension: No player controls their destiny alone! Your payoff depends on what others do (s_{-i}).
  • Our Ultimate Goal: View Algorithm Design as a Game between an Algorithm Designer (minimizing runtime/queries) and an Adversary (maximizing bad inputs).

Simple Examples of Everyday Games

Games appear everywhere across decisions, markets, and competition:

  • Rock-Paper-Scissors (Zero-Sum, Simultaneous):
    • You and your friend throw hands simultaneously. Pure conflict: +1, -1, 0.
  • Penalty Shootout in Soccer (Zero-Sum, Asymmetric):
    • Striker kicks Left/Right; Goalkeeper dives Left/Right. Conflicting goals.
  • Choosing a Meeting Location / Technology Standard (Coordination Game):
    • You and a friend want to meet up: Café vs. Library (both prefer being together over ending up alone at different places).
    • Or two cars approaching each other in a narrow alley: swerving to the same relative side allows safe passage; swerving into each other causes a crash!
  • Tic-Tac-Toe & Chess (Sequential Tree Games):
    • Alternating turns on a board with full visibility, no randomness, and no hidden information.

Mechanism Design: “Reverse Game Theory”

If Game Theory asks “Given the rules and payoffs, how will agents behave?”…

Definition: Mechanism Design

Mechanism Design asks the reverse question: “Given desired social goals, how do we design the rules, payment schemes, and payoff functions so that self-interested agents naturally achieve our goal?”

. . .

  • Also known as “Inverse Game Theory” or “Incentive Engineering”:
    • The designer cannot force players to act against their self-interest.
    • Instead, the designer engineers the payoff matrix / utility function: u_i(s) = v_i(s) - p_i(s) \quad (\text{valuation minus designed payment } p_i)
  • Famous Classic Examples:
    • Vickrey (Second-Price) Auction: Designing the payment rule such that bidding your true valuation is a dominant strategy!
    • Kidney Exchange & Stable Matching: Designing matching algorithms so participants have no incentive to lie about their preferences (Strategyproofness).
  • Takeaway: Payoffs aren’t always given by nature—often, we get to design them!

Game Representations: Tree Games (Extensive Form)

When decisions happen sequentially in time, we represent the game as a directed tree.

  • Backward Induction: Solve from leaves backwards to the root.
    • P2 chooses the branch maximizing their payoff at each subgame:
      • At left node: \max(1, 0) = 1 \implies chooses Left \implies payoff (3, 1).
      • At right node: \max(2, 4) = 4 \implies chooses Right \implies payoff (2, 4).
    • Anticipating P2’s rational response, P1 compares (3, 1) vs (2, 4) \implies chooses Left!

Perfect Information Games

A game has Perfect Information if every player, at every turn, knows the exact history of all prior moves:

Definition: Information Sets are Singletons
  • The current state is completely visible to all players.
  • No simultaneous moves, no hidden information, and no chance events (dice rolls).
  • In the game tree, each decision node is an information set of size 1 (players know exactly which vertex of the tree they occupy).
  • Examples: Chess, Checkers, Go, Tic-Tac-Toe, NIM.
  • Contrast with Imperfect Information: Poker (hidden hole cards) or Battleship (hidden ship coordinates) where players must reason under uncertainty!

Tree Games are Strictly Determined!

Theorem (Zermelo 1913, Kuhn 1953)

Every finite two-player zero-sum game of perfect information represented as a game tree is strictly determined in pure strategies.

. . .

  • What does this mean?
    • One player has a guaranteed winning pure strategy (or both can force a draw).
    • There is zero need for randomization or bluffing in Chess or Tic-Tac-Toe!
    • Backward Induction: The game tree collapses deterministically from leaves to root.
  • Why do we need Mixed Strategies?
    • When moves are simultaneous (or information is hidden), backward induction breaks down \implies randomization becomes mathematically essential!

Combinatorial Games: The Game of NIM

Two players take turns removing chips from piles. A pure combinatorial game of perfect information without randomness.

Rules of NIM
  • Start with k piles of chips: sizes c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k.
  • On each turn, a player selects one pile and removes any positive number of chips.
  • Normal Play Convention: The player who takes the last chip wins.

. . .

  • How can we solve who wins in polynomial time?
  • The state space is exponential (\prod (c_i + 1)), yet NIM is solved instantly using binary bitwise XOR!

Bouton’s Theorem: The Magic of Nim-Sum

Definition: Nim-Sum (\oplus)

The nim-sum of pile sizes is their bitwise XOR sum: S = c_1 \oplus c_2 \oplus \dots \oplus c_k

. . .

  • Bouton’s Theorem (1901):
    • If S = 0, the state is a P-position (Previous-player winning \implies second player wins).
    • If S \neq 0, the state is an N-position (Next-player winning \implies current player wins).
  • The Invariant Proof:
    1. From any state with S \neq 0, there always exists a move leaving S' = 0.
    2. From any state with S = 0, every legal move results in S' \neq 0.
    3. The terminal state has all c_i = 0 \implies S = 0 (winner just moved).

For 2 piles, $S = 0 \iff c_1 = c_2$ (Mirror Symmetry).
Winning move: Reduce Pile 1 from 5 to 3 chips to make both piles equal!
💡 Click any chip directly or use + / − to change counts • 🎯 2-Pile Invariant: Equal piles $(k, k) \implies S = k \oplus k = 0$ (P-position)!
  • Why does the Nim-Sum Invariant make 2-Pile NIM crystal clear?
    • For 2 piles: S = c_1 \oplus c_2 = 0 \iff c_1 = c_2 (Mirror Symmetry).
    • From any unbalanced state (c_1 \neq c_2): You can always reduce the larger pile to match the smaller one, forcing S' = 0.
    • From any balanced state (c_1 = c_2): Any legal removal breaks the balance, immediately giving S' \neq 0!

Simultaneous Moves: Bimatrix Games

When players move simultaneously (or without knowledge of each other’s choice), we represent the game in Normal Form.

The Two-Player Bimatrix Model
  • Player 1 (Row Player) has m strategies: i \in \{1, \dots, m\}.
  • Player 2 (Column Player) has n strategies: j \in \{1, \dots, n\}.
  • Payoff bimatrix (A, B) \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \times \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}:
    • If Row plays i and Column plays j:
    • Row Player receives A_{ij}, Column Player receives B_{ij}.
  • Zero-Sum Games: Special case where interests are purely antagonistic: B = -A \iff A_{ij} + B_{ij} = 0 \quad \forall i, j
  • General-Sum Games: Players can have shared interests, coordination, or mutual destruction.

The Classic Dilemma: Prisoner’s Dilemma

Two suspects arrested. Police offer each a deal independently:

Silent / Cooperate (C) Betray / Defect (D)
Cooperate (C) (-1, -1) (-3, \;\; 0)
Defect (D) (0, -3) \mathbf{(-2, -2)}
  • Strictly Dominant Strategy:
    • For Row: If Col plays C, Defect gives 0 > -1. If Col plays D, Defect gives -2 > -3.
    • Defecting (D) is strictly better regardless of what the other player does!
  • The Paradox:
    • Both players rationally play their dominant strategy D \implies outcome is (-2, -2).
    • Yet joint cooperation (C, C) gives (-1, -1)—better for both players!
  • Lesson: Individual rationality does not imply collective optimality.

Recap & Bridge to Next Lecture

Where We Stand
  • Game Models: Extensive Form (Game Trees, Zermelo’s Theorem), Combinatorial Games (NIM, Bouton’s XOR Invariant), and Normal Form (Bimatrix Games).
  • Dominant Strategies: When an action is optimal regardless of the opponent’s choice (e.g. Defect in Prisoner’s Dilemma).
  • The Central Paradox: Individual rationality does not imply global optimality ((-2,-2) vs. (-1,-1)).
