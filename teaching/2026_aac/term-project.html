Term Project

CS G526: Advanced Algorithms & Complexity · Aug–Dec 2026

Term Project Overview & Logistics

The CS G526 Term Project (accounting for 20% of the overall course grade) offers students hands-on engagement with advanced algorithmic concepts, either bridging foundational algorithmic theory or diving into research-level paradigms.

Team Format

Teams of 3

Total Weight

20% of Grade

Video Presentation

15 Minutes

Live Defense

3 Min Q&A

1. Project Format & Evaluation

Students work in groups of 3. Each team can either: 1. Choose an established topic from Track A (Foundation / Bridge) or Track B (Advanced Course-Aligned) below, or 2. Propose an original research / implementation topic (subject to prior instructor approval).

Deliverables & Timeline

Component Target Window Deliverable Details
Proposal Week 8 1–2 page PDF detailing team members, chosen topic/problem statement, preliminary bibliography, and milestones.
Video Presentation Week 13 A 15-minute recorded video presentation. Every team member must present. High-resolution slides + visual demos/benchmarks.
Live Q&A Defense Week 14 A 3-minute live interactive Q&A with the instructor and TA testing depth of understanding and technical contribution.
Final Report & Code Week 14 4–6 page report (IEEE / ACM / NeurIPS style) + clean GitHub repository with reproducible code, documentation, and tests.
Presentation & Q&A Guidelines
  • 15-Minute Video Recording: The recording should cover: problem definition, theoretical context, core algorithmic mechanisms/proofs, experimental design or architectural pipeline, and empirical results.
  • 3-Minute Live Q&A: Each student will be asked questions on the mathematical foundations, edge cases, and design choices of their project. Individual contributions will be evaluated during this defense.

2. Topic Pools

  • Track A: Foundation (Bridge) Topics
  • Track B: Advanced Course-Aligned Topics

Tailored for students who wish to reinforce their undergraduate core and establish solid algorithmic foundations before tackling research-level literature. These projects emphasize clean, pedagogical expositions alongside reproducible benchmark implementations.

A1. B-Trees & B+ Trees

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Why do databases and file systems universally rely on B-trees instead of balanced BSTs? Study multi-way branching, tree height bounds, insertion/deletion with recursive splits/merges, and leaf-linked B+ variants. Implement insert/search and build an interactive visualization of disk-block node splits.

References: CLRS Ch. 18; Ramakrishnan & Gehrke, Database Management Systems.

A2. AVL Trees vs. Red–Black Trees

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Two classic answers to maintaining balanced search trees. Derive the strict O(\log n) Fibonacci-based height bound for AVL trees, formalize rotation invariants, and experimentally benchmark tree rebalancing overhead under heavy insert/delete skew against Red–Black trees.

References: CLRS Ch. 12–13; Sedgewick, Left-Leaning Red-Black Trees.

A3. From Binary Heaps to Fibonacci Heaps

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Priority queues beyond binary heaps: d-ary heaps, binomial heaps, and Fibonacci heaps with potential-method amortized analysis. Implement Fibonacci heap decrease-key and empirically compare its wall-clock running time against 4-ary heaps inside Dijkstra’s algorithm on massive road networks.

References: CLRS Ch. 6, 19–20; Fredman & Tarjan (1987).

A4. Disjoint Set Union (Union–Find) & Inverse Ackermann

Track A Reading

The most elegant data structure in computer science: union-by-rank with path compression. Master Tarjan’s potential function proof achieving O(m \cdot \alpha(n)) amortized time. Explore applications in Kruskal’s MST, dynamic connectivity, and offline Lowest Common Ancestor (Tarjan’s LCA).

References: CLRS Ch. 21; Tarjan (1975), Efficiency of a Good But Not Linear Set Union Algorithm.

A5. Tries, Radix Trees & Suffix Structures

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

String-centric structures: standard prefix tries, space-efficient Patricia/Radix trees, suffix arrays with LCP arrays, and Kasai’s algorithm. Applications: substring pattern matching at scale, genome search, and longest repeated substring computation.

References: CLRS Ch. 32; Gusfield, Algorithms on Strings, Trees and Sequences.

A6. Skip Lists: Design & Empirical Comparison

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Probabilistic balancing: expected O(\log n) search, insert, and delete using coin flips. Implement randomized skip lists and perform head-to-head empirical evaluations against Treaps and balanced BSTs across concurrent or cache-locality microbenchmarks.

References: Pugh (1990); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 8.

A7. Comparison Sorts & Their Lower Bounds

Track A Reading

The \Omega(n \log n) decision-tree lower bound for comparison sorting, and algorithmic escape hatches: counting sort, radix sort, and bucket sort. Investigate when linear-time assumptions hold, integer sorting frontiers, and cache-oblivious sorting models.

References: CLRS Ch. 8–9.

A8. Dynamic Programming: Structure & Reconstruction

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

The rigorous DP paradigm: optimal substructure \to state recurrence \to memoization \to iterative tabulation. Focus on non-trivial reconstruction of solutions for Sequence Alignment, Edit Distance, and 0/1 Knapsack with space optimizations (Hirschberg’s linear-space algorithm).

References: CLRS Ch. 14–15; Kleinberg & Tardos Ch. 6.

A9. Greedy Invariants vs. Dynamic Programming

Track A Reading

What makes greedy algorithms provably optimal? Exchange arguments, the matroid theory framework for independence systems, and contrasting case studies where greedy fails catastrophically while dynamic programming succeeds (canonical vs non-canonical coin systems, interval scheduling variants).

References: CLRS Ch. 15–16; Kleinberg & Tardos Ch. 4.

A10. String Matching: KMP vs. Rabin–Karp

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Determinism meets randomization: prefix-automaton mechanics of Knuth–Morris–Pratt vs rolling-hash Monte Carlo Rabin–Karp (with false-match union bound analysis). Benchmark both across synthetic DNA sequences and natural language text.

References: CLRS Ch. 32; Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 1.

A11. The Amortized Analysis Toolkit

Track A Reading

A rigorous deep-dive into Aggregate, Accounting, and Potential methods. Apply all three paradigms across resizing vectors, multipop stacks, binary counters, and self-adjusting splay trees.

References: CLRS Ch. 17; Sleator & Tarjan (1985).

A12. Graph Traversals & Connectivity Toolkit

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Two simple traversals, immense consequences: topological sorting, 2-coloring, cycle invariants, and Strongly Connected Components (Kosaraju vs Tarjan SCC algorithms) with full correctness proofs and benchmark comparison on large web graph snapshots.

References: CLRS Ch. 22; Tarjan (1972).

A13. Shortest Paths Landscape: Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, Floyd–Warshall

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Proof mechanics for greedy relaxation, dynamic programming along path lengths, and all-pairs shortest paths. Address negative edge weights, negative cycle detection, and Johnson’s reweighting algorithm for sparse graphs.

References: CLRS Ch. 24–25.

A14. Minimum Spanning Trees: One Cut Property, Three Algorithms

Track A Reading

The universal Cut Property and Cycle Property. Formalize and compare Kruskal, Prim, and Borůvka’s algorithms. Study Borůvka as a parallel paradigm and analyze verification of spanning trees in linear time.

References: CLRS Ch. 23; Kleinberg & Tardos Ch. 4.

A15. Network Flows & Bipartite Matching

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Ford–Fulkerson method, Edmonds–Karp O(V E^2) BFS bound, Dinic’s blocking flow algorithm, and Max-Flow Min-Cut duality. Explore reductions to Maximum Bipartite Matching, vertex disjoint paths, and project selection.

References: CLRS Ch. 26; Kleinberg & Tardos Ch. 7.

A16. From Regular Expressions to Minimized DFA

Track A Reading + Light Implementation

Build a complete automata compilation pipeline: Thompson’s NFA construction \to Powerset Subset construction \to Hopcroft’s O(k n \log n) DFA minimization. Deliverable includes a functional regular expression parsing engine.

References: Sipser Ch. 1; Hopcroft, Motwani & Ullman Ch. 3.

A17. Context-Free Grammars & The CYK Algorithm

Track A Reading

Context-free grammars, parse trees, Chomsky Normal Form (CNF) conversions, and the O(n^3 \cdot |G|) Cocke–Younger–Kasami (CYK) dynamic programming parsing algorithm. Bridge parsing algorithms to matrix multiplication.

References: Sipser Ch. 2; Hopcroft, Motwani & Ullman Ch. 5.

A18. Turing Machines & The Limits of Decidability

Track A Reading

Turing machine models, deciders vs recognizers, Church–Turing thesis, diagonalization, and reduction proofs establishing the Halting Problem, Post Correspondence Problem (PCP), and Rice’s Theorem.

References: Sipser Ch. 3–5.

Directly integrated with the core themes of CS G526: Randomized Algorithms, Online Decisions, Game Theory, Approximation, Parallel Computation, Complexity Theory, and Streaming.

B1. Bloom Filter Engineering Study

Track B Implementation ●○○ Basic

Implement standard and counting Bloom filters with multiple independent universal hash functions. Quantitatively measure false-positive rates vs. bits-per-element across theoretical predictions (1 - e^{-kn/m})^k. Benchmark on high-throughput URL filtering or database cache admission.

References: Bloom (1970); Broder & Mitzenmacher (2004); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 8.

B2. Cardinality Estimation at Scale: HyperLogLog

Track B Implementation ●○○ Basic

Implement LogLog and HyperLogLog cardinality sketches utilizing stochastic averaging and harmonic mean estimation over leading-zero distributions. Evaluate empirical standard error against the theoretical 1.04/\sqrt{m} guarantee on multi-gigabyte text and network IP streams.

References: Flajolet et al. (2007); Heule et al. (Google HLL++, 2013).

B3. Cuckoo Hashing vs. Cuckoo Filters

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Implement two-table and d-ary cuckoo hashing with random walk eviction cycles. Contrast cuckoo filters (which store fingerprints instead of keys) with counting Bloom filters, evaluating insertion throughput, space efficiency, and deletion performance.

References: Pagh & Rodler (2004); Fan, Andersen, Kaminsky & Mitzenmacher (2014).

B4. Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) for High-Dimensional Similarity

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Build MinHash and LSH banding schemes for Jaccard similarity search across millions of documents or sentence embeddings. Empirically plot S-curves of collision probability against theoretical predictions and benchmark recall vs brute-force KNN.

References: Indyk & Motwani (1998); Gionis, Indyk & Motwani (1999); Leskovec, Rajaraman & Ullman Ch. 3.

B5. Karger–Stein Fast Min-Cut on Real Graphs

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Prove contraction tree branching probabilities for Karger–Stein’s O(n^2 \log^3 n) recursive algorithm. Implement the randomized algorithm with adjacency-list contractions and evaluate accuracy and speedups against deterministic Stoer–Wagner on SNAP graph datasets.

References: Karger & Stein (1996); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 10.

B6. Probabilistic Verification of Computations (Freivalds’ Technique)

Track B Implementation + Theory ●○○ Basic

Generalize Freivalds’ O(n^2) randomized matrix verification technique to verify polynomial multiplication, matrix inversions, and LU factorizations without recomputing. Evaluate empirical failure probabilities when intentionally perturbing low-rank matrix entries.

References: Freivalds (1979); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 1.

B7. Learning-Augmented Online Algorithms: Ski Rental

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Study learning-augmented competitive analysis where an untrusted machine learning oracle predicts future demand. Prove consistency (performance when predictions are accurate) and robustness (worst-case fallback when predictions are adversarial). Simulate using real cloud GPU spot-instance traces.

References: Purohit, Prasad & Svitkina (NeurIPS 2018); Gollapudi & Panigrahi (2019).

B8. Online Paging: Competitive Ratio vs. Empirical Cache Traces

Track B Implementation ●○○ Basic

Simulate FIFO, LRU, LFU, Flush-When-Full, and randomized Marking algorithms against offline optimal (Belady’s MIN). Evaluate miss rates on real memory memory traces and compare against the theoretical k-competitive deterministic and 2 H_k-competitive randomized bounds.

References: Sleator & Tarjan (1985); Fiat et al. (1991); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 13.

B9. The Secretary Problem & Matroid Secretary Generalizations

Track B Reading + Simulation ●●○ Intermediate

Derive the optimal 1/e stopping rule for the classical secretary problem via backward induction. Extend to multi-choice variants and the Matroid Secretary conjecture (Babaioff et al.), evaluating empirical performance on simulated auction matching markets.

References: Ferguson (1989); Babaioff, Immorlica & Kleinberg (2007).

B10. Solving Zero-Sum Matrix Games: MWU vs. Fictitious Play

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Implement the Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU/Hedge) method and Brown’s Fictitious Play to compute approximate minimax equilibria in zero-sum matrix games. Analyze empirical convergence rates to the game value V^* on security patrol and network routing games.

References: Arora, Hazan & Kale (2012); Freund & Schapire (1999); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 4.

B11. AdaBoost Derived from Multiplicative Weights

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Derive the foundational AdaBoost machine learning algorithm as an instance of the Multiplicative Weights Update rule over sample distributions. Implement AdaBoost from scratch and experimentally demonstrate margin maximization and training convergence on benchmark datasets.

References: Freund & Schapire (1997); Arora, Hazan & Kale (2012).

B12. Set Cover Three Ways: Greedy, LP Rounding & Primal–Dual

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Implement Chvátal’s greedy H_n-approximation, randomized rounding of the standard LP relaxation, and the primal–dual method for Weighted Set Cover. Construct worst-case integrality gap instances and evaluate empirical performance on benchmark hypergraphs.

References: Williamson & Shmoys Ch. 1; Vazirani Ch. 13–15.

B13. Metric TSP: Christofides Meets Modern Local Search

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Implement the 1.5-approximation Christofides–Serdyukov algorithm (MST + Minimum-Weight Perfect Matching on odd-degree nodes + Eulerian Tour). Combine with 2-Opt and Lin–Kernighan local search on TSPLIB benchmark instances to contrast worst-case guarantees with practical solution quality.

References: Christofides (1976); Williamson & Shmoys Ch. 2; Reinelt (TSPLIB).

B14. Knapsack: From Greedy to FPTAS

Track B Implementation ●○○ Basic

Compare greedy 2-approximation, exact pseudo-polynomial DP by profit, and the fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS) with scale factor K = \epsilon P / n. Empirically map the (1 - \epsilon) error guarantee vs execution time trade-off frontier.

References: Ibarra & Kim (1975); Vazirani Ch. 8.

B15. Vertex Cover: Rounding, Primal–Dual & Kernelization

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Study 2-approximations for Minimum Vertex Cover via LP rounding, Bar-Yehuda & Even’s primal–dual scheme, and Nemhauser–Trotter kernelization preprocessing (reducing instances using maximum bipartite matching). Benchmark graph size reduction factors.

References: Bar-Yehuda & Even (1981); Williamson & Shmoys Ch. 1, 7.

B16. Parallel Work–Depth in Practice: Prefix & Sorting

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Implement work-efficient parallel prefix sums (Blelloch scan), parallel merge sort, and parallel list ranking using OpenMP or modern thread pools. Empirically evaluate speedup and work-efficiency scaling on multi-core CPUs up to theoretical span limits.

References: JáJá, Introduction to Parallel Algorithms; Blelloch (1990); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 12.

B17. Byzantine Agreement under Adversarial Faults

Track B Implementation ●●○ Intermediate

Simulate oral-messages consensus protocols with f malicious/byzantine nodes. Verify empirically and analytically the impossibility bound n \le 3f, measuring network round overhead and message complexity as cluster size scales.

References: Lamport, Shostak & Pease (1982); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 12.

B18. Luby’s Parallel Maximal Independent Set

Track B Reading + Simulation ●●○ Intermediate

Prove the expected O(\log n) rounds bound for Luby’s randomized MIS algorithm. Build a distributed simulation on synthetic power-law and Erdős–Rényi graphs, comparing parallel convergence rates against greedy sequential MIS.

References: Luby (1986); Alon, Babai & Itai (1986); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 12.

B19. NP-Hard Reduction Pipeline to Modern SAT Solvers

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Select a non-trivial NP-hard problem (e.g., Exact Graph Coloring, Campus Exam Timetabling, or Hamiltonian Path), formulate a polynomial reduction to CNF-SAT in DIMACS format, and solve with modern CDCL solvers (MiniSat, Glucose, CaDiCaL). Benchmark against heuristic baselines.

References: Arora & Barak Ch. 2; Biere et al., Handbook of Satisfiability.

B20. Zero-Knowledge Proof Protocols in Action

Track B Implementation ●●● Challenging

Implement interactive Zero-Knowledge Identification protocols (Schnorr identification or Graph Isomorphism ZK). Build an interactive prover-verifier CLI demonstrate Completeness, Soundness against malicious provers, and Zero-Knowledge simulator indistinguishability.

References: Goldwasser, Micali & Rackoff (1989); Goldreich, Micali & Wigderson (1991); Arora & Barak Ch. 8.

B21. Approximate Counting in #P: DNF Counting & The Permanent

Track B Reading + Implementation ●●● Challenging

Explore FPRAS (Fully Polynomial Randomized Approximation Scheme) methods for #P-hard problems. Implement the Karp–Luby Monte Carlo algorithm for counting satisfying assignments of DNF formulas and contrast against exact model counters.

References: Karp & Luby (1983); Jerrum, Sinclair & Vigoda (2004); Arora & Barak Ch. 9.

B22. The PCP Theorem & Hardness of Approximation

Track B Reading ●●● Challenging

A guided theoretical tour of Probabilistically Checkable Proofs and gap-preserving reductions. Show how the PCP theorem leads to inapproximability results for Max-3SAT and Maximum Clique, and why Set Cover cannot be approximated within (1 - \epsilon)\ln n.

References: Arora & Barak Ch. 11, 22; Håstad (2001); Vazirani Ch. 29.

B23. Primality Testing: Miller–Rabin vs. AKS

Track B Implementation + Theory ●●○ Intermediate

Analyze error bounds of the randomized Miller–Rabin test based on Fermat’s Little Theorem and non-trivial square roots of unity. Implement both Miller–Rabin and the deterministic polynomial-time AKS test, benchmarking scalability across 64-bit to 4096-bit primes.

References: Rabin (1980); Agrawal, Kayal & Saxena (2004); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 14.

B24. Streaming Frequent Elements: Count-Min Sketch & Misra–Gries

Track B Implementation ●○○ Basic

Estimate heavy hitters and point queries over massive data streams with sublinear space. Implement Count-Min Sketch and Misra–Gries deterministic summary algorithms, evaluating precision/recall of top-K queries against exact counts on real network flow datasets.

References: Cormode & Muthukrishnan (2005); Misra & Gries (1982); Motwani & Raghavan Ch. 3–4.

3. Proposal Submission Template

Every team of 3 must submit a 1–2 page PDF proposal covering:

  1. Team Information: Names, BITS IDs, emails, and designated team coordinator.
  2. Topic Selection: Topic code (e.g. B10 or A3) or custom proposal title.
  3. Problem Formulation: Precise definition of the computational problem, theoretical significance, or engineering motivation.
  4. Planned Methodology:
    • For Implementation / Benchmarking: Programming languages, libraries/frameworks, dataset sources, and baseline comparisons.
    • For Reading / Theoretical Expositions: Key theorems to be unpacked, proof techniques, and pedagogical examples to construct.
  5. Milestone Schedule: Division of responsibilities across the 3 team members and week-by-week targets leading up to the 15-minute video and final report.
